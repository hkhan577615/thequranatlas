How to use the Atlas

أطلس القرآن

An atlas shows a whole land so you can see its shape, then lets you zoom in on one town. The Quran Atlas does the same for the Quran. It draws all 6,236 verses as pictures you can explore, and every picture opens back onto the verses themselves.

What the Atlas is

Every figure is counted from the Arabic text, word by word, using open scholarly data. The Atlas does not ask you to trust a number. It shows you where the number comes from, so you can tap through and read the verses yourself.

It is

A map of the text's structure: where words, roots, phrases, rhymes, stories and names fall across the 114 surahs. It is a way in for curious readers, students, teachers and people memorising the Quran.

It is not

Tafsir, a fatwa or a ruling on meaning. It does not replace reading the Quran or learning from scholars. Where a pattern depends on a choice, such as how to count a word or where a story scene begins, the Atlas says so.

Getting started

  1. Choose a view.The tabs under the title are the nine views. Each one shows the whole Quran from a different angle. Start with the Verse barcode if you're not sure.
  2. Tap anything.Every tile, dot, arc and bar is a verse or a group of verses. Tap one and a page rises from the bottom with the Arabic, an English translation (Saheeh International or Yusuf Ali), the root of each word, and a link to tafsir and recitation on Quran.com. Use Previous and Next to read on.
  3. Change the question.Most views have controls above the picture: colour by length or by Meccan and Medinan, mushaf order or revelation order, pick a surah, pick a root. Change one and watch the picture change.
  4. Read the notes.Under each picture is a short note on what it shows and how it was counted. Patterns are worth noticing, but a count is only as firm as the rule behind it.
  5. Read in Arabic or English.The عربي button at the top of the Atlas and the Library switches the whole site into Arabic. Each view has its own link, ending in #roots, #rhyme and so on, so you can send someone straight to it.

The nine views

Each view answers one question. There is a starting point under each to try first.

Barcodeبصمة المصحف

Every verse as one tile, laid out like a mushaf page from the top right. A gap marks each new surah.

Try: colour by Meccan / Medinan, then switch the order to Revelation.

Storiesالقصص

Where each prophet is named, and the story of every prophet, peace be upon them, broken into scenes, surah by surah.

Try: choose Musa and follow his call at the fire across the six surahs that tell it.

Namesخواتيم الآيات

The pairs of God's names that close verses, such as Forgiving and Merciful or Mighty and Wise, and what those verses talk about.

Try: open Forgiving and Merciful, the most common pair.

Ringsالنظم الحلقي

Raymond Farrin's reading of Al-Baqarah as a ring of mirrored sections, a check of it against vocabulary, and an echo map you can run on any surah.

Try: the echo map for Ar-Rahman, where the refrain shows up as a lattice.

Lettersالحروف المقطعة

The 29 surahs that open with letters such as Alif Lām Mīm, grouped into families, with the words that follow them and how often those letters recur.

Try: see how many openings are followed by a word for the Book.

Rootsالجذور

Search any of the 1,651 three-letter roots in Arabic, in Latin letters or by meaning, and see where it appears, the shapes it takes and the roots it travels with.

Try: type “mercy” or رحم.

Echoesالمتشابهات

Arches between repeated phrases across the whole text, the most repeated phrases, and near-identical verses that memorisers learn to tell apart.

Try: Confusable verses for a surah you are memorising.

Rhymeالفواصل

Every verse ending as a colour, one row per surah, showing where a surah holds one rhyme and where it shifts.

Try: Maryam, and watch the rhyme change as the story turns.

Voiceالالتفات

First, second and third person in every verse: I and We, You, He and They, and the turns between them that the rhetoricians called iltifāt.

Try: Al-Fatihah, where the voice turns from “Him” to “You” at verse 5.

Good to know

  • On a phone, swipe the row of tabs sideways to see all nine views, and turn the phone sideways for the wider pictures.
  • The first visit is the slowest, because the Atlas loads the full Arabic text and two translations, a few megabytes. After that your browser keeps them.
  • Dark mode follows your device setting.
  • Translations are meanings, not the Quran. The Arabic is always shown alongside them, quoted exactly.
  • Found a mistake? Corrections are welcome. A scholar's review of the children's books is still to come.
